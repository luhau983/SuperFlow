# Twelve Narrative Genres and Audiovisual Methods

This file merges each genre's director plan and storyboard table methods. Read the general usage rules first, then read the confirmed primary genre in full; when blending genres, also read the secondary genres actually adopted. Every genre's methods must be turned into concrete decisions for episodes, scenes, and shots; do not just paste the genre name into prompts.

## Usage and recording on the canvas

- The user's story, ending, aspect ratio, emotional tone, and confirmation records take priority. A genre is a choice of method, not a boundary that forbids mixing, says comedy can only be happy, or says xianxia cannot be sweet.
- The durations, fixed-camera ratios, music coverage, and counts below come from the old methods' pacing references and are not current model specifications. Re-weigh them according to episode length, dialogue, performance, and the actual model's duration/shot capabilities.
- Sub-second pauses, flash cuts, and black screens can be performance or post-production editing intent; when the model does not support them, do not submit invalid durations and do not invent a post-production tool out of nowhere.
- For continuous camera movement, spell out the start frame, path, new information along the way, and end frame. When multi-character interaction or long takes carry consistency risks, check real capabilities first, keep the story purpose, then choose to break it down, segment it, or realize it in post.
- "Little music" and "silence" must distinguish no music, ambient sound only, and total silence. The sound plan states sources and in/out points and does not assume the generation model can output every layer. The music methods below apply to existing music requirements or proposals pending confirmation; do not add music to projects that did not ask for it.
- Twists may use genuine partial views and point-of-view misdirection; hallucinations, dreams, and memories have recognizable markers. Do not fake objective images or add facts at the last minute to fool the audience.
- The script canvas's "narrative method decisions" record: primary/secondary genres, the feeling on leaving, passage beats, changes in character relationships/knowledge, signature actions, sound intent, and banned methods. It is kept separate from the formal script text.
- Each episode production canvas's "director plan" carries these decisions forward; shot by shot it realizes narrative purpose, new information for the audience, shot size and composition with reasons, action/reaction, camera movement start and end, duration basis, dialogue/sound, and transitions. Save executable content in the seven columns of the [storyboard table](../storyboardTable.md), with design reasoning in its accompanying notes; do not turn these knowledge dimensions into another table or tool fields.
- Merging only removes duplicate information; it must not delete functional reactions, shot/reverse shots, or pauses. Fewer shots does not mean better storytelling.

## Sweet Romance

**Core and beats.** Express relationship increments through restrained closeness: first-sight discovery → restrained secret crush → approaching then retreating → misunderstanding/separation → action resolving the knot → deeper closeness/commitment. Each push-pull round must change understanding or the relationship, not reset and repeat. Warmth within coldness, intimacy within distance; the climax can be denser emotionally while the editing stays unhurried.

**Information and performance.** The audience reads emotion from stolen glances, starting to speak then stopping, objects being passed, fingers approaching, breathing, and physical distance. First write what each side does not know or dare not express, then how actions leak it. Early on the polite distance is large; in the middle they approach but objects/crowds/hesitation block them; later the distance disappears once the relationship is confirmed. Concrete actions beat "very sweet".

**Shots and space.** Within a scene, go from medium shot to close-up, then extreme close-up when necessary; curtains, doors, windows, and foreground layers indicate separation, and reduced occlusion indicates intimacy. Save centered composition for rituals such as a formal confession; normally use thirds and relationship two-shots. A slow push-in means approaching/narrowing attention, a slow pull-out means withdrawal; about 60% static is an optional baseline. Wide shots carry loneliness or shared space and must not all be treated as wasted shots.

**Pacing, merging, and transitions.** Expressions 2–3 s, dialogue 3–4 s, entrances 3–5 s, wide shots 4–6 s can serve as a first pass; shorten long pauses with no new information. One or two establishing shots are enough; if a slow push-in can introduce the character at the same time, do not split it into three. A long take with sustained action and information can reference ≤12 s. Hard cuts within a scene, one environment/solitude segment as a buffer across scenes, and dissolves or fades only for major emotional shifts.

**Sound.** First meetings, mutual gazes, and turning away can drop the music and keep only ambient sound; set one tonal piece per emotional passage rather than changing tracks every scene. Everyday light instrumentals, a single low instrument for undercurrents, warm fade-ins for reconciliation, light percussion or plucked strings for heartbeat-like ambiguity. The old music coverage ≤60% is only for planning space; choose 1–2 recognizable ambient sounds per scene.

**Taboos and output.** Do not force sweetness with continuous fast cuts or wall-to-wall music, and do not overuse extreme close-ups of eyes and lips. The old version's turning hugs/kisses into partial shots was an option under generation limits; now such plot must not be cut or altered without consent. Write per scene "from what relationship state to what, how distance changes, when the key reaction is seen by the audience", then let the storyboard realize the full action or a feasible shot breakdown.

## Suspense/Mystery

**Core and beats.** A crack appears in a normal surface → anomaly discovered → investigation → a seemingly valid explanation → new evidence overturns it → backtracking to assemble the puzzle. The truth can have 3–5 layers; after releasing one valuable piece of information, leave a new concrete question; tighten—brief release—tighter, and do not substitute never answering for suspense. Give a questionable anomaly as early as possible; the old 30 seconds can serve as a short-form reference.

**Information design.** For each passage list the facts, what the audience knows/thinks it knows, what characters know, and what information is hidden. Misleading clues need a reasonable everyday explanation; important evidence appears at least twice, once "appearing naturally" and once "revealing its meaning". Flashbacks, inserted flashbacks, and parallel viewpoints are allowed, but each time-space jump has a clear visual/subtitle marker. Core twists are presented through prop state, the full view of the space, or reinterpretation of viewpoint; minor information can come through dialogue.

**Performance and clues.** Suspicious behavior wears an everyday cover, such as checking a watch, wiping hands, folding a letter; allow multiple interpretations, and do not pair each with a "villain face". Micro-expressions and paused fingers are clues, not a reliable lie-detection rule set. Each key-action shot focuses on one or two items, avoiding many busy actions that drown the evidence; unfinished sentences pair with meaningful silence.

**Shots and composition.** Medium shots show enough but hide the key part; close-ups serve justified attention to evidence; wide shots reveal the previously hidden whole. Clues can sit at the edge, in the foreground, or in a deep-focus background, but must be genuinely visible. An occluded slow push-in is peeping, a lateral move is searching, a slow pull-out is revealing, a sudden stop is discovery. Save Dutch angles for points of disorder, and centering or orbits for scrutiny/confrontation; do not shoot every shot as abnormal.

**Pacing and reveals.** Everyday 2–4 s; key discoveries 1–2 s longer than everyday; 4–5 s of pressure build-up before a twist; a reveal can hit in 1–1.5 s or unfold slowly over 5–6 s. Character reaction first and then what they see makes the audience wait; the audience seeing first and the character reacting later creates anxiety; once chosen, state the order. After a twist, leave 1–2 shots for digestion. Investigation is not blindly chopped into fragments; only chases suit acceleration; flashbacks can cut in on a matching action/object.

**Sound.** Everyday music mixes in faint dissonance; investigation uses a low rhythm or countdown-like ambient sound; before the twist pull the music, even to silence. Unknown sound sources can misdirect, but there must finally be a reasonable explanation of the source. For key scenes write one anomalous sound and when it returns/is revealed; voiceover only supplements necessary reasoning and does not replace evidence.

**Taboos and output.** Do not point arrows at foreshadowing with close-ups, overly long holds, or cue sounds; do not break the positional continuity of props; do not rely on newly added facts for twists. Creative decision nodes save "clue—first appearance—misreading—payoff", and the storyboard table saves precise prop states and reveal order; the final images carry no explanatory labels saying "this is foreshadowing".

## Hot-Blooded Shonen and Action

**Core and beats.** First establish what they fight for, then let crisis and real failure test their conviction; each round is build-up → tipping point → explosion → cost/breather. The low point needs loss and reflection, and growth is seen through moving from dodging to meeting the blow, from falling to standing up again. The climax can stack three layers: the lead standing, allies arriving, conviction paid off; overstacking cancels the force.

**Information and performance.** In a fight, first give both sides' goals, the gap in ability, and limits, so a comeback has traceable preparation. Show cost through wounds, trembling, fatigue, and a destroyed environment, not a character shouting "I've gotten stronger". Allies build trust through exchanged glances, covering each other's backs, and taking on risk voluntarily; a rescue can follow three beats: the lead in despair → sound/light intrudes → the ally revealed.

**Shots and attack-defense.** Low points from a high angle, standing up or payoff from a low angle; going from high to low angle within a scene can mirror the reversal. Wide shots convey positions and the direction of attack and defense, medium-close shots show action, close-ups focus on the tipping-point gaze/fist; save centered composition and orbits for a few heroic moments. Attack—defend—retreat—attack again has back-and-forth; organize the final blow in four beats: build-up, strike, impact, result; it cannot be all impact close-ups.

**Pacing.** Build-up 4–5 s, necessary slow motion about 6–8 s; impact 1–1.5 s; everyday 2–4 s. Slow, long build-ups followed by fast, short explosions create contrast; keep everyday warmth/humor as breathers in between. Fast push-ins convey power, following shots convey fighting side by side and pursuit, lateral moves convey speed; readability comes before shake. Continuous retreating then charging can be combined into a long take when spatial information is sufficient.

**Sound and dialogue.** In a crisis first pull the music, then give low pressure; 3–5 s before the explosion can keep only breathing/heartbeat, and the theme cuts in on the tipping-point action. Drums going from sparse to dense map the build-up; layers of fist whooshes, footsteps, gravel, etc. carry force. Choose declarations of conviction carefully; standing up can be silent; shouts and orbits are saved for a few true payoffs, not repeated with every move.

**Transitions and taboos.** Hard cuts within fights; brief memory inserts only supplement the present conviction; environment shots between passages let emotion settle. Flash-to-white/short black screens are editing intent, handled according to capability. Ban unjustified escalation, winning forever without cost, and shooting only chaotic melee without conveying space. Write per round "gap—preparation—failure/cost—choice—payoff" and the direction of force for the storyboard.

## Comedy

**Core and beats.** A character earnestly pursues a goal; clashing personalities and violated expectations produce comedy. Setup establishes normal logic → small accident → the fix makes it worse → chain of misunderstandings → loss of control → genuine feeling/return → one last leftover gag. Absurdity must have internal cause and effect; the more serious the character, the more effective, and the character must not laugh first on the audience's behalf.

**Gags and information.** Rule of three: the first establishes the pattern, the second reinforces it, the third changes it; on repetition the third must have a new difference. A misunderstanding chain states what A misunderstood, how B misreads A's reaction, and how much a third party knows. Embarrassment can escalate from making your own mistake, being seen, a failed fix, to collective loss of control; do not string unrelated bits together.

**Performance and blocking.** Write concrete contrasts, such as solemnly completing an absurd task; keep onlookers for the gags. Follow a gag with a reaction; in ensemble scenes each person can react differently, such as stunned, snickering, pretending not to see; when many lose control at once, use a wide shot so the audience discovers it themselves. Genuine-feeling passages reveal a character's vulnerability or kindness; do not rush to deflate every emotion.

**Shots.** Wide shots give the audience background the character does not know, medium shots carry personality interplay, close shots capture reactions, close-ups briefly magnify the wrong detail. About 60% fixed observation can be the base; a crash push-in magnifies a frozen reaction, a whip pan reveals absurdity, a slow push-in discovers detail, and following shots only when things spin out of control. If one shot can show a chain of failures, do not chop it up; with three identical compositions, change the third, so form also participates in the gag.

**Pacing and editing.** Setup 3–4 s, gag 1.5–2 s, reaction 2–3 s, and 1–2 s can be left after a laugh for digestion. A retort follows the previous line closely, with a slight pause before the gag; the old "no more than 60 seconds between laughs" is only for checking long absences of comedy and must not break necessary genuine-feeling passages. A chain of mistakes can use progressively shorter hard cuts; cutting from "I'll never do that" straight to doing it, or alternating plan and disaster, both form visual gags.

**Sound.** Absurd images get serious music or continuing normal ambient sound; a sudden full stop before an extremely absurd action creates contrast. Ambient sound can serve as a gag: an ill-timed phone ringing, a growling stomach; repeated sounds build a rhythm, broken the third time. Pull the music for genuine feeling, and avoid adding clown sound effects and canned laughter to every gag.

**Taboos and output.** Check for punchlines before the setup is established, gags without reactions, fast push-ins cutting off laughter, and flashy camera moves stealing the characters' scenes. Write "expectation—break point—reactor—digestion space—change across three repetitions", and let the storyboard realize information gaps and precise beats.

## Sci-Fi Apocalypse

**Core and beats.** Survival crisis → search for resources/shelter → testing trust → a brief glimmer → a greater cost → moral choice → continuing on wounded. Every action has a resource cost; trust goes from testing and cooperation to betrayal or loyalty under higher stakes. The ending can merely gain the possibility of going on, without forcing the world back to normal.

**Information release.** Use remnants of civilization instead of setting speeches: family photos, abandoned school buses, dead devices, and missing-person notices explain the life that once was. A recent apocalypse keeps traces of people having just left, a mid-term one has rust and weeds, a long-term one has collapse and vegetation cover; keep environmental decay consistent in time. Every arrival at a location gives resource, threat, and exit information at once; the environment is also an enemy.

**Behavior and choices.** Searching goes scan the space → approach the target → inspect the item → result or no result; a fight over resources shows the other side's fear and one's own hesitation together, avoiding shooting only the grab. Moral choice in three beats: full view of the situation, the character's struggle, the actual choice. Sacrifice uses the last mutual gaze, walking toward danger, and the reaction of those left behind; small kindness is precious because its cost is clear.

**Shots.** Ruins in wide shots with tiny human figures convey the scale of civilization; narrow passages, foreground debris, and tilted spaces convey pressure; close-ups see cracked lips, dirty hands, the last sip of water. Equipment gets a medium shot of the whole, then close-ups of damage; use extreme close-ups of relics sparingly. Micro-handheld rises and falls with danger, slow moves for searching, fast push-ins for crises, slow pull-outs revealing desolation from a detail; clear geography comes before a sense of chaos.

**Pacing and transitions.** Ruins 5–6 s, survival actions 2–4 s, crises 1.5–2 s, moral choices 4–5 s; exploration long takes can reference 8–10 s while continuously adding spatial information. Walking through the wasteland is the passage's breathing; it can go several shots without dialogue or music but still give migration/state changes. Hard cuts within scenes; across scenes use ruin segments, signal interference, or before/after decay of the same place; memories of civilization use a brighter texture to distinguish them from the present.

**Sound.** The absence of city/machine sounds that should exist expresses extinction; wind, metal, gas leaks, broadcasts with no signal, and intermittent alarms form a sparse soundscape. When deciding whether to abandon a companion, pull the music and keep only breathing and ambience; only a glimmer of hope lets a single warm instrument in. The old music ≤30% suggests restraint, not mechanical timing.

**Taboos and output.** Do not let resources replenish endlessly or injuries vanish without reason, do not use long character talks to explain the whole catastrophe, and do not pad atmosphere with meaningless empty ruin shots. Write per passage "resource remaining/cost—threat—trust change—moral cost—glimmer" and the environment's time state.

## Urban Workplace

**Core and beats.** Drive behavior with chains of interest: promotion, keeping a position, budget, professional reputation, and security. Advantage → opponent counterattacks via an event → on the defensive → find a breakthrough → small win/small loss → new cost; key turns land on concrete events such as emails, leaks, and resource decisions. There are interim results along the way, and the ending keeps a cost to relationships or principles, avoiding a costless comeback.

**Information and performance.** Write who holds what, who is cc'd, and the gap between public words and true intent. Knowing about a betrayal before the lead makes the audience anxious, knowing at the same time as the lead builds empathy; choose per scene. Meeting smiles, pressing a pen cap, hesitating to sign, deep breaths in the restroom show pressure; time alone and commuting are narrative for taking off the mask and switching roles, not just transitions.

**Space and shots.** The head seat, by the door, private office/open workstations, glass and partitions express power. Medium shots keep formal social distance, close shots imply trust or oppression, close-ups expose micro-expressions. A superior closing in/subordinate backing off, allies side by side/opponents across a table must all be supported by the story. Meetings go from wide shots to confrontational close shots, key subtext is followed by the listener's reaction; group speaking need not cut to each person.

**Camera movement and pacing.** About 65% fixed can be a calm, observational baseline, with very slight handheld when walking; slow push-ins scrutinize, slow pull-outs isolate, lateral moves show group relationships. Dialogue 2–4 s, silent standoffs 3–4 s, everyday fragments 1.5–2 s, alone 4–5 s; whether to extend a shot is judged by information change. A lateral move covering both speaker and reaction can merge shots, but key attack and defense must not be erased because of it.

**Sound and transitions.** Keyboards, air conditioning, paper flipping, coffee machines, elevators, and message vibrations give the space its identity; pull the music in key meetings and let silence apply pressure. Music is mostly a low drone or restrained instrumentals; the old coverage ≤40% is a reference. Everyday uses an alarm—commute—clock-in—overtime montage, a hard cut from the office to a quiet home shows the split, a new phone message can trigger the next passage; large jumps use clear time cues.

**Dialogue and taboos.** Lines can politely refuse/probe, but the listener's reaction must help understanding; silence after an important question marks who applies pressure. Use little voiceover to explain psychology, and avoid distorted industry detail, writing every struggle as a quarrel, or shooting the workplace like an inspirational ad. Write "interest motive—information access—power position—surface vs true intent—cost" so every change in a scene is traceable.

## Period Xianxia

**Core and beats.** Heavenly Dao/responsibility conflicts with personal feeling; fate is the starting point of choice. Prophecy or old cause → cultivation and bonds → trial of the heart → trial of the body → heavenly tribulation/ultimate choice → new enlightenment. Each enlightenment must change actions and costs, not just level up combat power. Past life, present life, and next life can form layers of cause and effect, but must match the confirmed source work.

**Information and performance.** Prophecies, celestial signs, tokens, and old memories appear in installments, and the audience waits to see how they are fulfilled; a master's gift to a disciple, the unspoken understanding of cultivation partners, and the stare of sworn enemies are more powerful than long explanations. Characters facing fate choose to accept or resist, and their behavior shows the move from obsession to letting go, from seeking immortality to questioning the heart. Poetic imagery must land as visible physical change, not just abstract words.

**Heaven, earth, and shots.** Space from the foot of the mountain to the summit can map cultivation realms; calm, wind and rain, thunder, and clouds parting can each echo emotional change, but do not add new laws on your own. Place tiny characters within large expanses of empty heaven and earth; cloud and mist occlusion and layered fore/mid/background add space; save centered composition for rituals. Wide shots speak of loneliness and scale, close-ups of tokens/spiritual power, extreme close-ups only when a tiny detail reveals the whole.

**Camera movement and combat.** Static or very slow at about 70% is a reference; slow pans like unrolling a scroll, slow push-ins entering the scene, slow pull-outs leaving the world, rises and descents corresponding to ascension/entering the world, flying follow shots steady and unhurried. Spells spell out the full intent of opening—charging—release; one move per shot rather than chopped fragments, and the finale can be charge—gaze—strike—pause—result. Escalation of celestial phenomena is marked per specific move, and effects must not obscure cause and effect.

**Pacing and transitions.** Mood 5–6 s, interaction 3–4 s, one move 3–5 s, enlightenment can be 6–8 s with natural dynamics such as wind, clouds, water, and light. Silent cultivation can span many shots as long as character/time/realm is changing. Clouds covering and dispersing, seasons cycling, water reflections, and dream dissolves handle space, time, and memory respectively; within the same scene clean hard cuts still work.

**Sound and dialogue.** Pine waves, waterfalls, wind chimes, and distant bells are the main soundscape; guqin, xiao, dizi, and xun play a phrase and pause; battles use drums, gongs, and bells; one key sword ring can be more powerful than wall-to-wall music. Save chanting for major rituals. Dialogue is concise and deep, poetic voiceover pairs with landscapes/wide shots; when blending genres, do not mechanically ban modern timbres.

**Taboos and output.** Do not turn xianxia into fast-paced monster bashing across the board, do not delete this genre's effective empty shots because of a generic "less imagery" rule, and do not replace choices with showy camera moves and screen-filling spells. Write "fate foreshadowing—character obsession—cost of each trial—enlightenment action—token/celestial payoff" and each passage's correspondence with heaven and earth.

## Historical Epic

**Core and beats.** Use individuals' choices in the torrent of the era to illuminate loyalty, life and death, the individual and the collective. The macro situation and micro characters interweave, accumulation → explosion → settling form large waves; each member of the ensemble has an emotional arc, converging at key historical nodes. Plan each person's "last time" glance back, raised cup, or standing side by side, so rituals come from irreversible meaning.

**Information and characters.** Do not restate background with long voiceover; consequences of orders, the collapse of order, common people's lives, and characters' trade-offs explain the era. White hair, new grass, children growing up, new people in old seats express time spans. Big choices can be made in silence; actions must explain the choice, not just solemn lines.

**Space and composition.** Court symmetry indicates order; standing/kneeling, high/low, front/back indicate power; equals at eye level; ensembles have a throughline focal character. After wide shots give the scale of the era, character close-ups serve as anchors of humanity; depth positions and small figures in emptiness speak of loneliness. Establishing shots should also convey the era's situation, avoiding only a place-name subtitle.

**Camera movement and duration.** Static or very slow at about 70% can be the baseline; overhead pans like a scroll of the era, slow push-ins focusing on choices, slow pull-outs revealing the whole from the individual, major rituals can witness without movement. Wide shots 5–7 s, standoffs 4–5 s, ritual actions 3–5 s, close-ups 3–4 s; battles rely on changes of space and shot size, not uniformly compressed into fragments. Marches, migrations, and court scenes can be covered continuously; long takes reference ≤15 s while continuously updating information.

**Sound and transitions.** Low instruments and drums carry weight, war songs/chants carry the collective, major decisions and aftershocks can keep only ambient sound. Battlefields separate close breathing and weapons, mid-distance voices, distant horns and hoofbeats; not a mass of noise. Empty shots serve as emotional buffers, imagery dissolves express the years, fade to black/fade in for turning large pages of history, hard cuts within scenes.

**Taboos and output.** Every ritual action must have meaning; do not replace weight with scale; do not replace choices with everyone giving impassioned speeches, and do not force an ensemble into a single-line payoff drama. Write "historical event—affected individual—choice—consequence for the era—personal cost", and the director plan then realizes macro-micro shifts, power positioning, and ritual actions.

## Family Warmth

**Core and beats.** Beneath the everyday cycle runs an undercurrent of gradual aging, imminent leaving home, or growing apart; everyday → clumsy care → generational disagreement → threat of separation → action and reconciliation → return/inheritance. The climax can be a meal back at the same table, without needing an explosive argument. Everyday moments that are "the last time" and children repeating their parents' actions bring out the weight of time.

**Information and performance.** Love leaks through habitual actions such as preparing an umbrella, adding rice to a bowl, leaving a light on, stuffing food into luggage; everyday talk is simple on the surface, and actions complete the emotion. The audience can see care the person involved does not see. The choices of two generations mirror each other, with both inheritance and difference; do not delete the repetition itself, but compare how the relationship changes within it.

**Objects and space.** Food conveys care, old objects convey time, keys and doors convey belonging. A full table, an empty seat, only two left is relationship change; kitchen, living room, and children's room each have a function. Medium/wide shots keep family members in the same frame, fore/background give silent watching, door and window frames give protection and departure; close-ups serve emotional evidence such as trembling hands, a suppressed smile, hot soup.

**Camera movement, duration, and merging.** Mainly static and steady slow moves, the old ~70% for reference; a slow push-in discovers a saved meal, a slow pull-out watches someone leave, following the carrying of a dish to the table can present the flow of life whole. Everyday 3–4 s, silence 4–5 s, details 2–3 s, watching someone leave 5–6 s. Dinner-table scenes need not cut to each person; homecoming or dish-carrying long takes reference ≤12 s, with speed following life rather than showing off.

**Sound and dialogue.** Stir-frying, kettles, slippers, and muffled TV form the home's rhythm; pull the music for key family moments and keep the sounds of life; "Have you eaten?" and "Call when you get there" paired with actions are enough. The old music ≤40% is a reminder of restraint; reconciliation can fade in warmly, and intergenerational echoes get the full theme. The sound of keys and the greeting on entering can repeat as an anchor of reassurance.

**Transitions, taboos, and output.** Morning-noon-evening, seasonal cycles, and everyday details handle transitions; large passages can slowly dissolve, hard cuts within a scene. Avoid piling on misery, forced tearjerking, lines summarizing the greatness of love, and fast cuts interrupting habitual actions. Write "repeated everyday—this time's difference—unspoken care—object/empty-seat change—reconciliation action", keeping informative ease and silence.

## Coming of Age

**Core and beats.** Around the clumsiness of a first crush, failure, farewell, or standing up for someone, growth goes "thought I understood → broken by facts → making a new choice". Everyday togetherness and shared experiences accumulate, then move toward separation and looking back; pain has a concrete cost, and sweetness and regret share an undertone of "time cannot be replayed".

**Structure and information.** An emotional line may string fragments together, and a dual line of the present looking back on the past is also allowed; each key change is bound to the weather, activity, or object of a specific "that day". Use onlookers such as teachers, friends, and parents to notice the lead's changes. Classrooms/sports fields build belonging, the road home carries secrets, the secret base carries promises; the same place going from side by side to walking alone shows the relationship.

**Performance and relationships.** Starting to speak then stopping, touching hands then pulling back, deliberately taking a detour then sneaking glances show clumsiness; in ensemble scenes each person has different small actions, and overlapping lines need not be neatly in turn. Physical distance from separate seats, side by side, each on one side, to coming close again can record the relationship state. Real growth may be no longer arguing or no longer chasing, letting silence and action speak.

**Shots and pacing.** Ensemble wide/medium shots give "being together", fore/background give one-way gazes, symmetrical side-by-side gives equal companionship; wide shots can mean freedom or loneliness, and the reason must be written. Alternate stillness and motion: following a run has vitality, a slow push-in notices change, a slow pull-out is farewell, a held shot is growth. Everyday fragments 2–3 s, emotion 4–5 s, epiphany 3–4 s, seasonal wide shots 4–6 s; the old static 50 / follow and slow push-in 30 / micro-handheld 20 is only an optional ratio.

**Merging and transitions.** Everyday fragment cuts have their own function and are not forcibly merged; confessions, quarrels, and reconciliations should develop continuously where possible; ensemble scenes keep the whole in one shot. Running/crossing-campus long takes reference ≤12 s with a change of mood. Time is contrasted through changes in the blackboard, seats, and leaves from the same angle; major emotional turns get empty-shot buffers, everyday moments use fast-cut montage.

**Sound.** Bells, sneaker squeaks, page turning, whistles, and bicycles form memory anchors; different arrangements of the theme melody evoke old emotions. Going from group revelry to a single voice can express farewell; pull the music at growth points. When voices turning laughter into tears or tears into laughter are enough, do not force music.

**Taboos and output.** Do not write perfectly mature teenagers, do not turn growth into utilitarian winning and losing, and do not replace experience with one lecture. Write "first time—misunderstanding—paying the cost—new action—that day/memory space", then make the camera's same-place changes and the sound echoes correspond.

## Horror/Supernatural

**Core and beats.** Normal safety → subtle anomaly → anomaly encroaching → false safety → worst premonition confirmed → anomaly lingering in the everyday. The threat enters the familiar space from afar/outside, then reaches one's side with nowhere to flee; the climax can be the danger appearing, or a change of understanding that "it has always been this way".

**Information control.** For each passage write how much the audience and the characters each know; the monster's full form or the source of the threat is delayed and revealed in installments, so knowing more is scarier. An extra chair, figure, or photo detail in a familiar space is more effective than gore out of nowhere. When real and unreal are hard to tell apart, mark the experiential viewpoint; the ending must be able to explain earlier anomalies, without altering reality without basis.

**Performance and shots.** First write bodily reactions such as freezing, holding breath, clenched fingers, slowly backing away, not screaming and running every time. Close-ups restrict peripheral vision, wide shots expose dangers the character does not know, door cracks/occlusion/out-of-focus depth and off-center negative space keep a potential threat. Something off can already be planted in the establishing shot; do not rush to give the full source of fear, reactions are often more effective.

**Camera movement and duration.** About 70% static is a reference; very slow push-ins close in, slow pull-outs reveal what is behind, movement stopping suddenly creates attention, a slight breathing feel can suggest being watched. Silence 3–4 s, reactions 3–4 s, atmospheric wide shots 5–7 s can serve a first pass; jump scares about 1–1.5 s, contrasting with the waiting. Exploring from corridor to room should keep continuous unease where possible; long takes reference ≤15 s with gradual information change.

**Sound.** Normal ambient sound → thinning → quiet → anomaly from an unknown source; false safety restores everyday sound, and the same anomalous sound returns at the end. Low dissonance and low-frequency timbres can be atmospheric design; do not treat a fixed infrasound frequency as a guaranteed fear effect. Use jump scares sparingly, building silence first; sound preceding the next scene can create a sense of intrusion.

**Transitions, taboos, and output.** Do not casually insert soothing empty shots into suspense-pressure passages; false safety has a separate clear purpose; hard cuts, short black screens, or sound-first transitions are used as the rhythm requires. Do not exhaust fear with sustained loud noise, consecutive jump scares, an early full reveal, or too much explanatory dialogue. Write "safe zone—anomaly evidence—what each side knows—false safety—confirmation—lingering anomaly", specifying shot by shot what is hidden and what is shown.

## Psychological Mind Games

**Core and beats.** Surface peace → probing → crack → awareness → turning the tables → deeper disguise → exposure. After each round's brief release the stakes rise, and each character's motives are understandable; the audience need not simply take sides. The cognitive layers can include "I know that you know", but the evidence must be enough to support it.

**Information and viewpoint.** For each passage make a three-party table: what A knows/pretends not to know, what B knows/pretends not to know, what the audience knows; choose a god's-eye view to see the trap, or a following viewpoint to be fooled together. Switching viewpoints must produce a cognitive update; pre-plant multiple independent details before a twist, and do not treat more layers as better.

**Dialogue and micro-actions.** Mark each line with its surface words, true purpose, and action: questions attack, evasion defends, counter-questions counterattack, silence applies pressure. Mark who creates the silence and who bears it; drinking water, paused fingertips, and shifting gaze can be misread and must not be treated as fixed lie detectors. Ambiguous answers must hold up in context, avoiding empty talk that pretends to be mysterious.

**Space and shot size.** Symmetrical two-person medium shots mean balance, a third person in a triangular composition introduces a variable, off-center or trapped in a corner means disadvantage; standing up, walking to the window, turning one's back change spatial power. Medium shots see body language, close shots/close-ups read the cracks, wide shots see the overall situation, extreme close-ups only for key clues. Each shot/reverse-shot switch is a round of attack and defense; do not force a two-shot just to reduce shot count.

**Camera movement and pacing.** Core confrontations are static, the old ~60%–65% as a reference; very slow push-ins apply pressure, slow pull-outs reveal the whole, slight drifts show the scales tipping, and only the exposure may change shot size abruptly. Dialogue 3–5 s, silent standoffs 4–6 s, micro-expressions 2–3 s, twists 1–2 s; actual pauses follow the lines and the thinking process. When movement through space drives psychological change, long takes can reference ≤12 s.

**Sound and transitions.** Peaceful passages get light underscoring or no music, probing fades in low dissonance, showdowns suddenly pull the music, and stalemates keep only ambience and breathing. Warm timbres can accompany a character's false front, but must ultimately have a basis; a heartbeat cannot always equal lying. Round or viewpoint changes use clean hard cuts, short flashbacks supplement the clue being seen through in the present, scene changes keep the tension, and characters get time alone to think when necessary.

**Taboos and output.** Every shot must carry an information layer; do not interrupt the reading of expressions with functionless atmosphere or pervasive fast cuts; do not rely on evidence suddenly appearing at the end to prove someone knew everything all along. Write "round goal—each side's hidden cards—audience position—double meaning of lines—micro-actions—who owns the silence—how the advantage shifts", and the storyboard is organized in complete rounds.
