# Story development: turning ideas into causal action

Used for creating from scratch, diagnosing an existing story, complex stories that need an outline, and continuing a sequel. It supplements the writing methods in [Input and script](../sourceAndScript.md) and adds no production stage or confirmation gate.
For a finalized script, extract production material directly; the methods below can be used to locate execution doubts, but cannot become a reason to automatically rewrite characters, plot, dialogue, or ending.
The methods provide judgment tools; they do not require the work to have positive growth, a twist, a suspense ending, or a particular genre. Cited examples are method examples, not facts of the current project.

## 1. First separate facts, proposals, and unknowns

When to use: when receiving a story, fragment, or sequel material, and when about to add a motive, rule, or answer during writing.

1. From the current scope, find the events that actually happened, what characters said, and established relationships and rules, keeping the source.
2. Distinguish "the character believes so" from "the story has confirmed so"; unreliable narration, rumors, and character lies cannot be registered directly as truth.
3. List newly added motives, dialogue, and causal bridges created to connect the plot separately as proposals; identities, letter contents, and mechanisms the author has not revealed remain unknown.
4. Handle gaps only when they affect the current choice. Proactively fill creative ideas that can reasonably be proposed; for factual gaps, state that the basis is insufficient; do not ask the user to fill out a full questionnaire.

Handed to the next step: a working draft that distinguishes known basis, creative additions, and retained suspense; it can be attached directly beside the current script, without creating a separate report.
Common misuse: inferring "the letter is the father's last words" from "she puts away the letter", then writing those last words into the climax; this answer must have another source or be an explicit proposal.

## 2. Focus the idea with a story proposition

When to use: when the user only has a mood, a subject, or a one-line premise, or when the existing story has many subplots and its core is hard to state.

First find the question the audience should keep caring about this time, then write a working sentence: what kind of person must accomplish what, blocked by what, and losing what if they fail.
Land the abstract goal on one recognizable action; "pursuing freedom" can become "moving out his own workbench before the family comes back", but the new action is still a creative proposal.
Then write the other half of the proposition: why the character chooses, and from what state to what state. The theme is proven by the result, not replaced by a speech at the end.
If the user has no direction yet, give a few distinct situations for them to express preferences; if they already have an interest, develop it, rather than repeatedly inventing competing stories.

When there is no event, you can find a step in an everyday task that cannot be completed as usual, then ask who handles it, why they hesitate, and how handling it once affects others. First try writing one decision by the character; if a concrete conflict remains after deleting the novel nouns in the premise, this direction has a basis for further development.
Teaching example: a new canteen worker swaps the labels on two batches of lunches and wants to quietly swap the boxes before serving; while checking special meals, he finds the outer packaging alone cannot restore them, so he has to halt serving and ask the shift leader to check together.
What needs developing next is how he takes responsibility for delaying the meal and how colleagues respond, not adding an even bigger accident. The example only demonstrates drawing a choice out of a task mistake.

### Let viewing conditions affect concrete trade-offs

First keep the known release purpose, viewing mode, and the audience's familiarity with the subject; without a basis, write the judgment as a working assumption. A platform name cannot directly imply "the audience only accepts fast pacing", and an age label cannot replace concrete needs.
Work backward from the experience this piece wants to leave: viewers meeting the characters for the first time need to understand what is happening early; viewers familiar with the previous work more need to know what changed this time. For mobile viewing, check whether small text and several people talking at once are hard to make out; passages relying on hesitation, awkwardness, or loss need to keep reaction time. Turn these judgments into which interaction to keep, which explanation to simplify, and which result to make clearer, rather than writing a separate audience profile.
Story scale should also match the available length: if the main experience comes from two people learning to work together again, prioritize developing that interaction; extra characters or background take time only when they affect choices.

### Turn reference works into testable writing choices

When the user provides a reference work, first state the specific effect being borrowed, then explain how it is achieved based on what was actually read or seen. Narratively, you can observe when the audience understands the task and when they revise their judgment; visually, you can observe how attention is arranged and how the environment affects action. Do not fill unread parts into analytical conclusions.
Then return to this project, design an approach for the same expressive purpose that fits the existing characters, events, and space, and check whether it works. Do not transplant the reference's character relationship combinations, signature premises, continuous plot, dialogue, or shot sequences; and do not treat titles and style labels as completed analysis. When no reference is needed, develop the current story directly.

Handed to the next step: a one-sentence action proposition, one main audience question, and the value change to be tested at the ending, shared by character and event design.
Common misuse: treating "courage, love, redemption" as a proposition, or using more proper nouns to make a premise with no direction of action look complex.

## 3. Diagnose from where it does not read through or does not hold attention

When to use: the user asks for an evaluation, the material is rich but the short's main line is unclear, or adaptation requires judging what is already in place.
After reading the agreed scope, first restate what the character is dealing with using existing events, and mark where you start being unable to explain the next step, unable to tell characters apart, or no longer care about the result. Diagnosis centers on these concrete positions; do not apply the same set of scoring columns to every story.

If it is unclear why a character acts this way, go back to before this decision: is what he wants to change clear, are the information he holds and his habitual ways enough to explain the decision, and does the other party actually hinder it. Write down the basis that can be found in the text; not having read a motive does not prove the character has none, nor can it be filled in on the author's behalf.
If a lot happens but nothing progresses, pick two adjacent important actions and check whether the later one depends on the result of the earlier. Then imagine another character performing the same action: if the cause and result are unchanged, the character's traits may not yet participate in the plot; if removing a passage affects no choice or necessary experience, it may be repeated development. From this, locate the specific causal break, stray goal, or character function; do not delete slow passages across the board.
If you know what happens but lack anticipation, find a change still to come among the unfinished actions, contradictory relationships, or abnormal results already in the text, and explain why the audience would care. It can be whether a task gets done, or how a conversation will end; a gentle opening in the original does not mean there is no usable opening material.
If curiosity turns into confusion, follow one important piece of information to trace how it is discovered, hidden, misread, or confirmed. Check separately the basis on which characters and audience receive it, and what decisions it changes once known; keep it unknown when the answer has not been provided. A work with a public goal may have no secret; do not break an already established action to manufacture an information gap.

Land findings on the affected events, distinguish "existing content cannot support it" from "this material has not provided it yet", and point out the material that is still valid. Conclusions rest on textual basis, not on grades or scores.
When the user only wants a diagnosis, deliver findings and basis; when the user also authorizes development, continue fixing the specific breaks and mark the added content.
Handed to the next step: which valid conditions to keep, which problem to handle first, and its scope of impact; do not create a "cannot continue until diagnosis passes" threshold.
Common misuse: only giving labels like "weak characters, slow pacing" without pointing out which decision lacks basis; or forcing a secret into a gentle relationship story to add suspense.

## 4. Shape characters through goals, flaws, and choices

When to use: the protagonist can only passively endure events, characters are interchangeable, or the climax choice lacks believable motivation.

1. Write the external goal: what exactly to obtain, prevent, prove, or keep this time, and when it counts as success.
2. Write the internal need: what the character may need to admit, give up, or take on; it can conflict with the external goal or stay aligned with it.
3. Write the old strategy: how he habitually solves problems, why it worked before, and why it causes loss this time.
4. Turn the flaw into behavior and consequence, e.g. "fear of losing control" shows as making decisions for companions, so companions stop offering help.
5. Arrange a real choice under pressure, letting the audience recognize the person from behavior; appearance, labels, and backstory can only explain behavior, not replace it.

Do not force trauma, secrets, or growth arcs onto every character. A steadfast protagonist can also change those around him through choices; tragedy can come from refusing to change.
Build key supporting characters by their function this time: who blocks the goal, provides information, offers another choice, bears the result; one person can carry multiple functions.
Handed to the next step: the character's current goal, habitual strategy, key relationships, and basis for choices, enough to support scene writing; a full biography is not needed first.
Common misuse: only writing "cold outside, warm inside"; add a contradiction that affects this plot, such as refusing cooperation verbally but having kept a tool aside for the other person in advance.

## 5. String events into causality, then arrange escalation

When to use: the synopsis reads like an event list, repeated setbacks do not advance, or the protagonist suddenly gets a solution.

First write "character's expectation -> action taken -> actual consequence -> strategy forced to change to". The gap between actual consequence and expectation must come from the opponent, established rules, or the character's misjudgment.
Connect the main events with "because, therefore, but"; flashback can change the telling order, but the causes of events must still hold.
Opposing forces are not limited to villains; environment, time, institutions, relationships, and the protagonist's old strategy can all apply pressure. Choose only layers that interact; do not force three layers of conflict.

| Changeable condition | How it forms escalation rather than repetition |
|---|---|
| Time and space | The window of opportunity shrinks and retreat closes; a problem that could be solved slowly now demands an immediate choice |
| Resources | Tools, evidence, or help are depleted, forcing the character to use another method |
| Information | New facts overturn old judgments, so the original plan may harm the real goal |
| Relationships | Cooperation comes with new conditions, allies begin to doubt, solving the external problem costs the relationship |
| Cost of failure | From recoverable inconvenience to concrete loss, accumulated from earlier decisions |

For example: a caretaker hides a malfunction to save an old clock -> a colleague starts it as usual and breaks a part -> he must disclose the maintenance records he hid to prevent it being started again.
Each step changes his available actions; merely running into three strangers' obstruction in a row increases the number of obstacles but does not necessarily constitute escalation.
Handed to the next step: an event chain whose causes can be traced, and the goal, resources, relationships, or understanding that each turn changes.
Common misuse: adding a new ability near the end to save the day; key rules and problem-solving resources should be established in advance, but not all their uses need to be explained in advance.

## 6. Complete the climax and theme with a costly choice

When to use: the climax has only spectacle, or the ending explains the mystery but has no character outcome.

Return to the proposition, find the two things the character least wants to lose at the same time, then check whether the pressure really makes them hard to have both.
The crisis provides the cost, the choice exposes the ranking of values, and the climax shows the action and consequence of the choice; do not just have the character say "I've decided" and cut to the result.
Earlier material should establish why the cost is personal and concrete. A public admission of a mistake can weigh more than large-scale destruction with no emotional connection.
The ending answers the main waiting question and contrasts with the opening through relationships, situations, objects, or actions; tragedy, stagnation, or contradictory outcomes can equally work.
An open ending need not answer how things ultimately turn out, but the character's choice or emotional process this time must reach a meaningful position; it cannot simply cut off before the climax.

Handed to the next step: the final choice, visible result, necessary emotional pause, and questions intentionally kept open.
Common misuse: one option has no cost and the other is obviously wrong, yet the character is claimed to face a dilemma; and do not tamper with a finalized script to manufacture a dilemma.

## 7. Allocate time for advancing and lingering according to the current difficulty

When to use: events exist but the center of gravity is unclear, or different passages compete for length. First choose the one change the current story most needs the audience to experience, then find the premises needed to understand it, the process by which the change happens, and the time to bear its consequences. Genre names cannot directly decide how these passages unfold.

First try writing this change as a complete scene, observe where it actually gets stuck, then handle the corresponding problem:

- **The action ends too easily, and what follows can only keep adding things.** State clearly the conditions for completing the task, and check whether the existing environment and characters would really prevent completion. Danger should change the available routes or ways of coping; rules should become limits on a specific action. A disadvantaged person must face concrete differences in qualification, resources, or power, and fight for room with abilities the story has established. Give obstacles and responses enough length; do not solve it with sudden help from a stranger.
- **Every attempt looks the same.** Follow one remedy to trace the problem it leaves behind: how a misjudgment makes the next attempt harder, and who bears the loss for a convenience gained. When writing temptation, first let the benefit hold, then let the cost of using it enter the next decision; when writing a chain of mistakes, let consequences come from what the character just did. Both cases need a change of strategy; do not merely enlarge the scale of the accident.
- **The result is clear, but the character seems not to have lived through it.** Compare how he acts when facing similar situations, and land the internal change on one different choice. Relationship changes must affect the ways of cooperating, refusing, or depending; when an identity is hidden, revealed, or used, it must change the conditions of his interactions with those around him. Leave time to recognize old habits, new understanding, and actual interaction; do not treat chases or big twists as necessities.
- **The reader only waits for an explanation, and the middle has nothing to watch.** Give characters who are investigating or waiting a current task. Each new piece of information should make some judgment hold or fail and affect subsequent action; before explaining the answer, first make sure the audience already knows what is worth asking about. If the work advances through emotion rather than puzzles, leave the time for communication and observation; there is no need to manufacture secrets.

These treatments can be interwoven in the same scene, or not needed at all. When reviewing length, prioritize keeping the necessary basis before a choice and its consequences; when compressing repeated attempts, do not erase the change of strategy along with them. A story with small, clear events can finish quickly, while complex choices may need to be built across scenes; do not distribute time evenly by number of shots or dialogue lines.
For works with weak causality, fragmentary, or observational works, check instead the internal order of perspective, emotion, recurring imagery, or theme; do not automatically rewrite them because they are unconventional.
Handed to the next step: the leading audience question, the turn most in need of development, and the emotional landing point, for the outline and time estimate to allocate length.
Common misuse: treating the symptoms above as stages the story must go through and adding scenes one by one; or deleting the pause that is exactly what makes a choice hold, for the sake of "stronger pacing".

## 8. Write an outline when needed, letting structure carry the handoff

When to use: the story spans multiple stages, character relationships are complex, or the user asks to see the structure first; a short, clear story can go straight to script, and a finalized script does not need an outline filled in retroactively.

First group events by stage goal, without presetting the number of groups. For each group, write the key events, character actions, resistance or cost, the ending change, and how it leads to the next group.
Then check character functions and relationships: what each side wants, what resources or information each holds over the other, and how the relationship changes after this event.
You can express it in text like "A needs B's permission; B depends on A's skills; after the malfunction is made public both lose the basis for cooperation", without adding a document just to draw a relationship map.
Check whether a passage can be deleted: if a key cause, basis for choice, necessary experience, or later payoff would be lost, keep it; passages that only repeat the same function can be proposed for merging.
An emotional pause can itself be a necessary experience; do not delete it across the board on the grounds of "no new information".

Handed to the next step: a coherent synopsis, necessary characters and their functions, relationship changes, and an event structure grouped according to actual need; keep existing positioning and duration constraints.
Common misuse: adding events to fill five sections, or inventing appearance, backstory, and unconfirmed answers at the outline stage, forcing later assets to inherit them.

## 9. Sequels continue from the adopted state

When to use: sequels, later segments of the same work, or reusing old characters and locations; read only the history the user specified and material actually relevant this time.

1. Extract from history the events that happened, relationships, rules, timeline, prop ownership, injuries, and spatial state, marking the adopted version.
2. Compare with the current content and record each as carried over, new, changed with reason, conflicting, or missing; new ideas are not written as history.
3. Write changes as "previous state -> triggering event this time -> subsequent state", neither freezing characters nor resolving contradictions by resetting history.
4. Inherit the stable style and character foundations, and re-check this episode's goal, opening question, and closure; a task solved in the previous episode does not automatically become this episode's goal.
5. When the historical script differs from adopted segments, point out the specific impact and keep undetermined parts pending; do not arbitrate by file timestamps on your own.

Keep plot changes and asset changes separate: a breakdown of trust may only affect performance; only damaged clothing may need a corresponding appearance state. Missing unrelated historical files does not block current creation.
Handed to the next step: this time's starting point, historical facts that cannot be rewritten, the range of new events, reusable states, and motivated changes, for script and asset extraction.
Common misuse: continuing after reading only the last line of the previous episode, or re-inventing identity, personality, and world rules to introduce old characters.

## 10. Turn internal judgment into usable results

Choose the methods above by problem; do not require an item-by-item report. Generally deliver one current story direction: the protagonist's action, key causality, choice, and result; attach an outline and substantive proposals when necessary.
Complete revisions directly within the explicitly authorized revision scope; for substantive changes beyond the scope, explain the impact and use the workflow's existing confirmation method, without adding per-method confirmation.
When ready to write audiovisual text, go to [Screen adaptation](screenAdaptation.md), turning the event chain into scene tasks, complete actions, dialogue, and sound; later production only reads the currently valid script and does not inherit unadopted creative branches. If the target is Seedance, go directly to PART A of the main `SKILL.md` after the script is finalized.
