# Screen adaptation: making the story happen in image and sound

Used for converting novels and synopses into scripts, and for authorized script revisions. When the story still lacks a main action or ending, use [Story development](storyDevelopment.md) as needed; a full set of upstream documents is not required.
A finalized script keeps its plot and complete dialogue by default. The checks below are used to explain production needs and find conflicts; changing events, information reveals, character motives, or dialogue lines is an adaptation proposal, handled per [Input and script](../sourceAndScript.md).
Each method is an internal writing tool, not an item-by-item submission or approval checklist. Examples only show how to write, and cannot be treated as existing facts of this project.

## 1. Map source evidence to audiovisual expression

When to use: the novel has a lot of narration, background, or psychology, or compressed adaptation easily drops necessary information.

First extract this time's character goal, obstacle, cost of failure, and key change, then judge item by item how it is established in the source and how the audience learns it in the script.
Record two layers, event facts and telling order: switching to flashback may not change events but changes the audience's judgment and expectation, so the timing of reveals must be checked.
For each item, you can choose to perform it directly, express it through evidence in existing actions, keep a necessary voiceover, or propose merging and compressing; do not assume all literary expression should be deleted.

| Source content | Check when choosing the mode of expression | Result handed to the script |
|---|---|---|
| Events happening now | Who did what where, and what changed | Actions with start and end states |
| Character psychology or relationship judgment | What triggers it now, how it affects choices, what consequences can be seen | Visible reactions and strategy changes |
| Past experience or world rules | Which choice this time must depend on this information, and what evidence the source has | Dialogue, objects, sounds, or brief voiceover appearing as needed |
| Unresolved information | Whether it may be revealed this time, and how much characters and audience each know | Keep it unknown, or reveal it on a clear basis |

### Build anticipation from what actually happens in the opening

First write one concrete event the audience can see or hear on entering the work, and check the minimum information needed to understand it. Sound, text, or objects can come first, but they should let the audience recognize what is happening, not leave only an incomprehensible stimulus.
Then state what change the audience will wait for: whether an action can be completed, whether two people will keep talking, what consequences an anomaly brings; all can create anticipation, and the answer need not be hidden. Finally connect to the character's next action, and check whether this opening really starts or changes this piece's task; if deleting the opening has no effect, check whether it is just an extra gimmick.
Teaching example: as a laundry is about to close, the clerk finds the number of coats awaiting pickup does not match the pickup slips, and the doorbell rings right then. The audience knows someone has come to collect clothes and will care how the clerk handles the missing item; if what follows turns to an unrelated date, the waiting established by the opening has no follow-through.
Include the recognition, reaction, and action time the opening needs in the time estimate; do not apply a fixed number of seconds. Checks on a finalized script are for explaining expression needs; reordering the opening or revealing information earlier is still an adaptation proposal.

Handed to the next step: where necessary facts are carried in each scene, and a concise list of added, deleted/merged, and reordered content.
Common misuse: background written only into character cards, with no channel of expression in the final film; or creating a flashback that changes the plot just to replace one line of voiceover.

## 2. Distinguish scene task from character goal

When to use: a scene is just meeting, explaining, and leaving, or there is a lot of dialogue but no change.

First determine what will be different after this scene ends, then write the action the character actually needs to complete right now. A scene can get two people to start cooperating while the characters themselves are just trying to move a bookshelf into the room; the plot function should happen through the process of this task.
Write the goal with a verb first: make the other stay, retrieve the record, stop the signing, confirm an identity; it should ideally be possible to judge success or failure within the scene; avoid writing only "gain understanding".
Then write resistance and reactions: the opponent also has an immediate purpose, and the environment or rules also put concrete limits on action.
Organize the scene as "goal -> obstacle -> action -> reaction -> strategy adjustment -> result", establishing action first and then writing dialogue.
At the end, mark which of situation, relationship, resources, information, or choice has changed, and how the result causes the next scene; change can be gradual, and every scene need not flip.

Teaching example: two roommates move a bookshelf; one insists on lifting the whole shelf over the threshold but cannot turn it because of the weight; the other suggests unloading the books and shimming the casters to the same height. The first lets go of the top edge he has been gripping and pushes the base as the other directs, and only then do they get the bookshelf inside.
The task's obstacle gives the character a chance to give up directing alone, and cooperation is established through actual action; if this trust is needed later, there is already a traceable basis.
Handed to the next step: one action chain per scene, the main change, and the end state, as the basis for dialogue, asset states, and later storyboards.
Common misuse: making every scene explain the setting, develop relationships, plant several foreshadowings, and deliver a big twist, so no task is developed enough; a necessary pause or observation scene also need not have an argument forced in.

## 3. Trace evidence of psychological change back from later behavior

When to use: when conclusion sentences carrying key progression appear, such as "he finally understood", "she lost trust", "he was driven by desire".

First see which later action depends on this psychological judgment. For example, to have characters carry out a plan together, the audience must believe he has accepted the other's participation. Merely adding a smile to his face may not explain this change.

1. Find the limit still in effect before the change: the character is unwilling to hand over a certain decision, does not believe a certain piece of news, or insists on a certain approach. The basis comes from behavior already written; habits not in the source cannot be turned around and treated as adaptation facts.
2. State clearly what possibility exists after the change: the other can participate, something starts being used, something previously avoided is finally dealt with. First determine the actual difference, then choose an action sufficient to show it from the characters and objects already in the scene; do not apply gestures by emotion name.
3. Return to the moment of change and check what new evidence the character received and what consequences he met. If these experiences are not enough to change him, repair the reason or slow down the change; do not let one action prove out of nowhere that he has come around.
4. Write the process back into continuous performance. Whether the other can notice and respond, whether the existing interaction changes accordingly, and whether later scenes carry over the result should all be readable. A pause can express hesitation, and the use of space and objects can land a choice, but add them only when they serve the current causality.

Teaching example: the original sentence is "she begins to trust the new colleague". In the earlier scene she always checks shipments alone; the new colleague finds a size error and on his own initiative sends it back for remeasurement. When packing again, she hands half the stack of orders to be checked to him and continues with the other half herself; the division of work that follows makes trust an actual decision.
The basis for this passage is the earlier checking behavior and the current division of work; when added actions are used in a real adaptation they should be marked as proposals. If, when reading, the change can only be known from the word "trust", keep checking whether what is missing is the experience, a visible decision, or later follow-through; do not solve it by adding expression adjectives.

Handed to the next step: performable key behaviors, necessary reaction time, and before/after states of props and space.
Common misuse: converting every line of emotion into a small action with no consequence; or deleting voiceover that has a narrative function in the source work, leaving only hard-to-read silence.

## 4. Arrange information gaps and reveal timing

When to use: suspense, misunderstanding, identity, or relationship reveals depend on "who knows what", or the audience feels confused.

First write the real content of the information and its basis, then record what character A, character B, and the audience each know, wrongly believe, or do not know at this moment; unknown answers cannot be filled in automatically by this table.
Choose the information relationship by the desired experience; the audience need not always know a bit more or a bit less than the characters.

| Information relationship | Suitable effect | Execution points |
|---|---|---|
| Audience knows first | Worry, anticipating the character's discovery | Give the audience clear evidence, give a reasonable cause for the character not knowing, and let later actions approach the consequence |
| Character knows first | Curiosity, reinterpreting the character | The audience at least knows which question is worth asking, the character's concealment fits their purpose, and do not stall by deliberately withholding necessary images |
| Both learn at once | Shock, empathy, pressure to choose | Give time to recognize and react to the information, then change action; do not stop at explaining the mystery |

Place important information where it will affect the next judgment or choice; you can also show the result early to create suspense, but check whether it breaks the original perspective or mystery.
A twist should reinterpret existing clues. Misdirection can come from a character's misjudgment, incomplete evidence, or a reasonable double interpretation; it cannot make objectively shown facts invalid for no reason.
After each reveal ask: who changes strategy, trust, or risk judgment because of it next; if no one is affected, it may only be extra explanation.
Handed to the next step: each passage's audience question, information source, reveal position, and action consequence, which can be injected directly into scenes without delivering a separate table.
Common misuse: hiding all information so the audience does not even know what the protagonist is doing; or having characters refuse communication that would obviously complete the task just to prolong suspense.

## 5. Write purposeful dialogue with subtext

When to use: characters always state their feelings directly, dialogue repeats the image, or different characters' lines are interchangeable.

Start from the actual consequence this exchange aims to cause: after the character speaks, whether the other stays, tells the truth, or accepts an arrangement. You can first write the direct request internally and test whether it fits the current relationship; if saying it directly has no cost, there is no need to talk around it.
When it truly cannot be said directly, find how the reason for the blockage enters the language. For example, someone afraid of rejection may use an unfinished task to prolong time together, and someone wanting to regain the initiative may answer only part of the other's question. The chosen wording must rely on facts within the scene, letting the audience infer intent from the mismatch between words and actions; do not explain a vague line with a newly invented backstory.
Continue writing how the other understands and responds, then let the speaker persist, change their words, or stop based on this response. Avoidance, mismatch, interruption, and silence arise from this exchange and cannot be inserted mechanically; task information that must be conveyed clearly is still stated clearly. Wording, speaking speed, and pauses keep the known personality and voice requirements.

Teaching example: the father is noting the portions needed for the next day's breakfast, and the daughter's luggage stands by the door.

```text
Father, pointing at the note: Two servings again tomorrow morning?
Daughter, sticking the recipe on the fridge: This is enough for you for three days.
Father waits a moment, then changes the "2" on the note to "1".
```

The father uses preparing breakfast to probe whether she will stay, and the daughter lands her answer on arrangements for him living alone; the father changing the portion shows he has received this answer. The luggage and what they are doing give the exchange a basis for interpretation.
Dialogue check, in order: does it change action or judgment, does it repeat the image, did the other already know, could a more precise action carry it.
Handed to the next step: complete dialogue, speakers, necessary actions, and pauses; keep OS, VO, and on-screen sound distinct, and do not treat textual voice descriptions as real audio assets.
Common misuse: mechanically shortening every line and breaking the character's rhythm, or rewriting finalized dialogue on your own to pursue "more subtext".

## 6. Make props and space resources for action

When to use: relationships rely entirely on explanation, the location feels like an arbitrary background, or objects appear and disappear between scenes.

Work back from this scene's action to which objects are truly irreplaceable: if it is taken away, who cannot complete the next step, and which basis of understanding the audience loses. Things that must be fought for should have conditions for obtaining them; things that carry a relationship or message should have an established origin, and their meaning cannot be declared by the writer in an explanation.
Then trace the process of use, including who holds it, whether it is usable, when it is exposed, and how it is handed over or damaged. If later material changes its use or reinterprets it, earlier material must leave a recognizable basis. Not every furnishing needs symbolic meaning.
For space, first write the functional relationships: who can enter, who controls the exit, whether sound carries between areas, what blocks the two people, then decide how action changes these relationships.
For example, two people across a counter compete for the same receipt, and the clerk removes the divider in between so both sides can check the signature; the spatial change turns confrontation into cooperation, more visibly than a voiceover announcing reconciliation.

Handed to the next step: the asset's necessary use, start and end states, holder, and important spatial relationships; extract only needs that actually appear, and do not add assets for a metaphor.
Common misuse: an object used only to look good being treated as a key clue; or using "she moved closer" to mean trust without stating whether it affects the ongoing interaction.

## 7. Organize scene rhythm with action and reaction

When to use: conflict starts too late, explanation continues after the result is already clear, or events are so dense there is no time to feel them.

Try entering late and leaving early: enter at the moment sufficient to understand the conflict, leave after the key result and necessary reaction are established, and compress functionless entrances, small talk, and repeated endings.
A decision in an earlier scene can cut straight to its execution in a later scene; before omitting travel and waiting, first confirm that no necessary change in location, time, props, or what characters know will be lost.
Important reveals can land on reactions; if the consequence has not yet been understood by the audience, keep the reaction and result rather than cutting away immediately for a fast pace.
Check the main progression each passage carries, and its role in action, relationship, information, and necessary experience; repeated functions can be merged, but do not mechanically cut scenes on the rule "must satisfy two items".
Slow-paced passages should also have a direction of observation, e.g. repetition of the same action gradually exposing change. Fearful waiting, grieving pauses, and comedic pauses each have their own time needs.

Handed to the next step: a clear scene entry point, ending result, necessary pauses, and transition basis. If the later target is Seedance, after the script is confirmed hand it directly to PART A of the main `SKILL.md`; shots and segmentation are not designed further in this method.
Common misuse: cutting every scene down to only the result so the audience cannot see how characters choose; or using frequent cuts to hide action that repeats in place.

## 8. Estimate time by actual development

When to use: initial estimate after the synopsis is formed, calibration after dialogue and actions are complete, and when a hard duration and the content seem to conflict.

1. List the causality, relationship information, key choices, results, and necessary experiences that cannot be lost this time, and first judge which narrative passages are needed.
2. Estimate separately the time needed to naturally finish the dialogue, complete actions, recognize on-screen information, react with pauses, and transition. When there are no details early on, give a range and state the assumptions.
3. Mark which parts happen simultaneously and which sequentially: the same passage where speaking and doing can happen together can overlap; parts where text must be read before reacting should be added up in sequence.
4. Calibrate complex dialogue and actions with a read-through or actual performance; speech rate, character count, and number of scenes are only aids, not a fixed conversion formula.
5. Total the current scope and check against known hard limits; even without a user duration requirement you can proactively estimate, without making the user bear technical time estimation.

For example, if a stretch of dialogue is tentatively estimated at 12 seconds, and the character seals a box throughout while speaking, taking 8 seconds, this overlapping passage counts as about 12 seconds; then reading the seal takes 4 seconds, reacting 2 seconds, transitioning 2 seconds, for a total of about 20 seconds.
If sealing the box can only start after the dialogue ends, you cannot take the larger value and must add them in sequence instead. This example only illustrates the timing method and does not represent a fixed performance speed.
Time estimates must distinguish the time the story needs, the time a single model run supports, the total duration of generated media, and the final edit duration; media can overlap, and a model's upper limit does not prove the story fits.

Handed to the next step: total duration range, main time-consuming passages, and assumptions. If the later target is Seedance, this is only a script-level time estimate input; actual generation segmentation, per-segment duration, and Batch are decided only by PART A A2/A3 of the main `SKILL.md`.
Common misuse: estimating a summary line like "the two fix the machine" at a few seconds based on its few words, or adding all parallel dialogue and actions together, inflating the estimate.

## 9. Keep the basis and consequences of choices when compressing

When to use: the estimate exceeds constraints, passages repeat, or the user explicitly asks for something tighter; for finalized content, only propose a specific compression plan, and do not change the plot on your own.

Check from smallest to largest impact: functionless entrances, exits, and waiting -> repeated explanation and identical conflict -> mergeable scene tasks -> subplot development -> important events and ending.
The first items must also be checked for whether they carry necessary emotion, clues, or spatial continuity; "merging scenes" is not a cost-free operation and may change who overhears a secret.
For each compression point, state what is deleted or merged, where the saving comes from, what experience is lost, and how causality is kept; do not just write "faster pacing".
Core motives, rule setup, key choices, information reveals, and their consequences cannot be squeezed in merely by swallowing words, fast playback, or deleting reactions. If it is already tight, state clearly that there is no safe room for compression.
Compare a fuller development with a compressed development only when there is a substantive trade-off, and recommend the version suited to the current content; do not force two sets of timings or two sets of ideas every time.
If the hard limit cannot accommodate it, give concrete plans for extending, splitting, or adjusting the scope along with their impact, using the existing confirmation process; do not quietly change the ending or split episodes.

Handed to the next step: the adopted execution scope, time estimate, and preservation boundaries; later work must not mix events and timings from different versions.
Common misuse: keeping all event names but deleting the choices and reactions between them, so it seems "all the content is there" but actually only a plot summary remains.

## 10. Write producible events and continuity clearly

When to use: the script is about to enter the asset, storyboard, and media stages, or involves multi-person action, frequent costume changes, multiple locations, and large-scale changes.

For each key passage, first identify the main visible event, then write its cause, key action, and result, avoiding cramming multiple simultaneously changing characters, objects, and spaces into one line.
Whether a complex set piece is kept depends on its dramatic value and actual production conditions. Capability limits must be verified; do not treat "close-ups are always easy to generate" or "ensemble shots cannot be done" as fixed facts.
Without authorization to change the plot, do not alter events on your own in this method. If the later target is Seedance, how actions are carried, how shots are broken down, or segmentation is decided by PART A of the main `SKILL.md`.
Which scene a state change happens in, who triggers it, and how long it lasts afterward should all be findable in the script; character appearances and objects without changes continue to carry over.
Keep the sound, on-screen text, OS/VO, and hearing conditions necessary to the plot; the script need not be filled with shot sizes, camera movements, or generation parameters, and later stages choose the expression based on each scene's task.

Handed to the next step: a current script with scene numbers, location and time of day, characters, complete actions and dialogue, necessary sounds, and start/end states; asset needs can be traced back to this content.
Common misuse: deleting key crowds or changing locations on your own for generation convenience, or treating unadopted appearance proposals as state changes that have already happened in the script.

## 11. Do one continuous read before delivery

Read once along the character's experience, confirming what he wants now, how he acts, what result he gets, and why he moves to the next step; then read once from the audience's perspective, checking the basis for understanding and the timing of reveals.
Check that key psychological changes have audiovisual evidence, important props and rules are established, complete dialogue and necessary pauses fit within the time estimate, and the ending fits the current scope.
Bring only substantively unresolved items and adaptation additions into the delivery; do not write all internal checks into a separate report. When the user only asks for production, keep the original draft and explain the specific execution issues.
If the currently valid script enters Seedance production, hand it directly to the embedded PART A of the main `SKILL.md` to finalize shots, segments, sound, and final prompts; do not automatically inherit this document's examples or unconfirmed adaptation branches.
